You are extracting marketing claims from a vendor's website content, for
later verification against real customer reviews on G2.

A marketing claim is a specific, checkable statement the vendor makes about
itself or its product. Do not include vague slogans or adjectives with no
checkable content.

REVIEWABILITY -- this is the most important filter. Before including a
claim, ask: "could a G2 reviewer plausibly confirm or refute this from their
own experience using the product?"

Keep claims about user-facing experience and outcomes: support quality,
ease of use, performance, results or ROI a customer achieved, specific
feature behavior.

Outcome statistics such as "customers acquire 129%% more leads" or "close 36%% more deals" ARE reviewable even though a reviewer can't independently
verify the exact percentage -- a reviewer CAN confirm the direction of the effect (did leads/deals/resolution time improve after adopting the product?). Keep these.

Drop claims that are abstract business-scale or customer-base statistics,
even when the number is impressive, because no individual reviewer's
experience can confirm or refute them: total customer counts ("299,000+
customers in over 135 countries"), community size ("1.4M+ community
members"), or market-share style statistics ("62%% of Fortune 100 use
[product]"). These describe the vendor's overall business, not something any
single reviewer experiences -- skip them even if they sound like strong
marketing claims.

FEATURE-DESCRIPTION SENTENCES ("Build marketing campaigns from one
AI-powered workspace that unifies planning, creation, and execution.") are
only reviewable, and should only be kept, if they name a SPECIFIC, CONCRETE
mechanism or capability that a user could point to and say "yes, this
actually works well" or "no, it doesn't." Drop feature-description
sentences that only use generic/soft language with no specific mechanism
named -- these read as taglines, not checkable claims, even though they are
phrased as feature descriptions. For example, drop: "put tedious tasks on
autopilot while your team focuses on strategy" (no concrete mechanism
named), "transform scattered conversations into meaningful relationships
with a unified inbox" (too generic about what the inbox actually does), and
"manage website personalization with unified tools that scale tailored
content" (same generic "unified tools" pattern, no specific mechanism).

VERBATIM TEXT -- claim_text must be the exact, complete, intact sentence as
it appears on the page. Do not paraphrase, summarize, shorten, or combine
multiple sentences. Do not extract a fragment or sub-part of a sentence. Copy
the full sentence exactly as written, including its original punctuation.

IMPACT SCORE -- for every claim that passes the filters above, assign an
impact_score from 1 to 10 estimating how much this claim would influence a
prospective buyer's purchase decision. Score higher: claims with concrete,
differentiating detail (specific proven outcomes, named mechanisms with real
behavioral detail, third-party rankings) that a buyer comparing options
would find persuasive. Score lower: claims that are true and reviewable but
generic, minor, or easily true of most competing products too. A claim
having a number in it does not automatically mean a high score -- score on
how differentiating and decision-relevant the claim is, not on whether it
is quantified.

The input contains multiple pages, each starting with a line in the form
"### Page: <url>". For every claim you extract, set source_url to the exact
url from the "### Page:" marker of the page it came from.

Return every claim you find across all pages that passes the reviewability
filter above, each with its impact_score. Do not limit how many claims you
return -- scoring and selecting the final set happens after your response.
